How it all began

A failed fundraiser that changed everything

In 2012, our founder Jonathan Reed watched a $40,000 charity drive vanish into admin fees — and a village in Ghana still drink from a muddy river. So he built the opposite: a giving platform where every dollar is tracked, every project is photographed, and every receipt is public.

The first Stackly well cost $6,800. It still runs today — with a photo of every donor who made it possible painted on its tank. That well became a wellfield, then a school, then a health clinic. The model never changed: radical transparency, local leadership, and proof you can see.

  • Public quarterly impact reports
  • Field teams in every region
  • Projects named for their givers
  • Community-owned from day one
“People don't stop giving because they stop caring. They stop giving because they stop trusting. We exist to make trusting easy.” — Jonathan Reed, Founder

What we stand for

The four values we never negotiate

They are printed on our office wall, baked into our contracts and audited every single quarter.

01

Radical transparency

Every dollar gets a receipt, a photo and a GPS pin. If we can't show it, we don't spend it.

02

Local leadership

Communities design their own projects. We fund, train and get out of the way.

03

Zero-waste giving

96 cents of every dollar reaches the field. Overhead is funded separately by our board.

04

Dignity first

No poverty porn, no savior stories. Just partners, progress and respect.

The journey

Fourteen years, six turning points

2012

One well, one promise

Stackly registers as a 501(c)(3) after funding the Bongo well in Ghana — and posts every invoice online.

2014

First 100 water projects

The 100 Wells Challenge completes ahead of schedule across three countries, serving 210,000 people.

2017

Classrooms, not just wells

Education launches in partnership with 14 local ministries — 9,200 children enrolled in year one.

2020

Emergency response unit

When crisis strikes, we're on the ground within 72 hours — food, shelter and medical aid at zero overhead.

2023

One million lives

The millionth person served receives the keys to a new community health post in Kiberia, Nairobi.

2026

Today — 32 countries

12,500 active volunteers, 40,000 monthly donors and a simple rule that never changed: show everything.
